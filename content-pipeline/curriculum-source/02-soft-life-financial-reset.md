# The Soft Life Financial Reset — Full Curriculum

**Price:** $149 · **Tier:** Single course · **Modules:** 6 · **Lessons:** 13

**The promise:** by the end of this course, a learner has a calm, honest picture of where her money actually stands, a budget she can keep for longer than a week, a plan for her debt, a real emergency fund started, and a one-year map toward the goals that matter to her.

**Who it's for:** a woman who feels like she "should" have her money together by now, who has avoided looking closely at it out of shame rather than not caring — this course replaces the shame with a system.

**Note before recording:** this course gives frameworks and habits, not individualized financial, tax, or legal advice — say so plainly in the welcome lesson and again in the workbook intro, and encourage learners with complex situations (significant debt, self-employment taxes, major life changes) to pair this with a licensed professional.

## Module 1 — Money Without the Shame

*Framing: nothing in this course works until the shame spiral is interrupted first. This module is short on purpose — it's permission, not homework.*

### Lesson 1.1 — Naming Where You Actually Stand (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a complete, honest snapshot of her current money situation in one place for the first time.

Teaching outline:
- Open by naming the pattern: most people don't have a money problem, they have a "won't look at it" problem — avoidance costs more than any single bad decision does.
- Walk through gathering the four numbers that make up a real snapshot: total in checking/savings, total debt across every account, monthly income, and monthly essential expenses.
- Give permission for this to be uncomfortable the first time and reframe it as data collection, not a report card — a doctor needs the numbers before she can help, not judgment.
- Show where to actually find each number quickly (bank apps, a debt tracking app, or a simple manual list) so this doesn't turn into a multi-day project.
- Close with the snapshot fully filled in as the one deliverable of this lesson — everything else in the course builds from it.

**Opening line:** "We're not judging the number today, we're just finally looking at it."

### Lesson 1.2 — Untangling Money From Self-Worth (8 min)

**Objective:** the learner can name at least one inherited money belief that isn't serving her and one true statement to replace it with.

Teaching outline:
- Introduce the idea of "money stories" — beliefs picked up early, often not even consciously, from family, culture, or past hard times, that still drive decisions now.
- Give common examples to help viewers recognize their own: "money is the reason my parents fought," "wanting more than enough is greedy," "I'm bad with money" repeated so often it became identity.
- Teach the separation: a bank balance is a number, not a character reference — the goal of this course is skill-building, and skills can always be learned regardless of the current number.
- Offer a short reframing exercise: name the old belief, name where it likely came from, write one truer sentence to replace it.
- Close by connecting this directly to the rest of the course: every remaining lesson is the "how," this lesson was the "why now, and why it's safe to look."

**Opening line:** "Your bank balance is not a report card on who you are — let's untangle those before we touch a single budget line."

**Workbook — Module 1: Money Without the Shame**
- Money snapshot: fields for total savings, total debt (by account), monthly income, monthly essential expenses, and one blank line for "how it feels to see this written down."
- Money story worksheet: "A belief about money I inherited is ___," "It probably came from ___," "A truer sentence is ___."

## Module 2 — The Calm Budget

*Framing: most budgets fail because they're punitive. This module builds one that's built to actually survive a real month.*

### Lesson 2.1 — A Budget That Doesn't Feel Like a Punishment (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner understands the difference between a restrictive budget and a calm one, and can name why past budgets didn't stick.

Teaching outline:
- Open with the honest confession most viewers share: they've "tried budgeting" before and it lasted two weeks — name why, usually because it was built around deprivation instead of reality.
- Teach the calm-budget principle: a budget's job is to tell money where to go on purpose, not to forbid every non-essential purchase.
- Cover building in joy spending as a real line item from day one, not an afterthought or a guilty exception — a budget with zero room for a coffee or a treat is a budget that gets abandoned.
- Walk through the emotional side of past budget failures briefly, so this doesn't feel like a moral failing repeating itself.
- Set the tone for Module 2: the next lesson gives the actual system, this lesson just clears the ground so it can stick this time.

**Opening line:** "If your last budget felt like a punishment, that's not a you problem — it's a budget problem. Let's build a calmer one."

### Lesson 2.2 — Building Your Three-Bucket System (13 min)

**Objective:** the learner has her actual monthly income sorted into three working buckets, sized to her real numbers.

Teaching outline:
- Introduce the three-bucket framework: needs (non-negotiables — housing, food, minimum debt payments, utilities), future (savings and extra debt payoff), and life (everything that makes life feel good — the joy spending from 2.1).
- Give realistic starting percentages as a guide, not a rule (roughly 50/30/20 as a common starting split), and show how to adjust the split for her real numbers from the Module 1 snapshot rather than forcing an unrealistic ratio.
- Walk through actually assigning her real monthly expenses into the three buckets, line by line, using her snapshot from Lesson 1.1.
- Address the common snag directly: what to do when "needs" alone already exceeds what feels sustainable — this isn't a failure, it's information for Module 3 and 4 (debt and goals).
- Close with her three-bucket totals fully filled in as this lesson's deliverable, ready to track against for the rest of the course.

**Opening line:** "Three buckets, one system — let's sort every dollar so you always know where it's supposed to go."

**Workbook — Module 2: The Calm Budget**
- Past-budget reflection: "A budget I tried before was ___," "It stopped working because ___."
- Three-bucket worksheet: monthly income at the top, then Needs / Future / Life columns with space to list each expense and its bucket, plus a running total per bucket.
- Joy-spending line: one line specifically protected for guilt-free spending, with an amount she commits to.

## Module 3 — Debt, Decoded

*Framing: debt is a math problem wearing an emotional costume. This module handles the math and the feelings, separately and in order.*

### Lesson 3.1 — Listing It Without Flinching (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner has every debt she owes listed in one place with balance, interest rate, and minimum payment.

Teaching outline:
- Normalize the fact that most people have never seen all of their debt in one list — it's usually scattered across apps, mentally avoided, or only half-known.
- Walk through gathering the four fields per debt: who it's owed to, current balance, interest rate, and minimum monthly payment.
- Address avoidance directly and gently: it's common to not know the exact interest rate on a card — show her where to find it in five minutes rather than guessing.
- Reinforce the Module 1 tone: this list is data, not a verdict — the size of the list has no bearing on her worth or her ability to handle it, starting now.
- Close with the full list built, sorted for now by balance size (highest to lowest), setting up the payoff-method decision in 3.2.

**Opening line:** "Let's get every single debt on one page — not to feel bad about it, just so we can finally make a plan against it."

### Lesson 3.2 — Snowball or Avalanche: Choosing Your Method (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner picks a debt payoff method and can explain why it fits her personality and numbers.

Teaching outline:
- Explain both payoff methods clearly: snowball (paying off the smallest balance first for quick emotional wins) and avalanche (paying off the highest interest rate first to save the most money).
- Give the honest tradeoff: avalanche is mathematically optimal, snowball is often psychologically sustainable — and sustainable beats optimal if optimal gets abandoned in month two.
- Use her list from 3.1 to model both methods side by side so she can see her own actual payoff order under each approach.
- Cover the "extra payment" concept: whatever amount from her Future bucket is going toward debt gets applied to the target debt while minimums are still paid on everything else.
- Have her commit to one method and mark her real payoff order in the workbook.

**Opening line:** "There's a mathematically perfect way to pay off debt, and there's the way you'll actually stick to — let's find yours."

### Lesson 3.3 — Talking to Creditors Like an Adult (11 min)

**Objective:** the learner knows what to say and ask for when contacting a creditor about her debt.

Teaching outline:
- Normalize that calling a creditor feels intimidating but is a completely normal, common request that these departments field constantly.
- Cover the three most useful asks: a lower interest rate, a hardship or payment plan if she's struggling to meet minimums, and clarity on any fees she doesn't understand.
- Give an actual script structure to follow: state the account, state the ask plainly, and be ready to hear "no" and ask "is there anything else you can offer" before hanging up.
- Address negotiating from a place of calm confidence rather than apology — she's a customer asking a reasonable question, not asking for a favor she doesn't deserve.
- Close with her identifying one real creditor call she'll make this week, with her ask written out in advance.

**Opening line:** "Picking up the phone is scarier in your head than it is in real life — here's exactly what to say."

**Workbook — Module 3: Debt, Decoded**
- Full debt list: creditor, balance, interest rate, minimum payment — as many rows as needed.
- Method choice: snowball vs. avalanche comparison table pre-filled from her list, with her chosen method circled and her real payoff order written out.
- Creditor call script: account name, the ask, a backup ask, and a line for the date she plans to call.

## Module 4 — The Safety Net

*Framing: an emergency fund isn't about fear, it's about never having a bad week turn into a bad year. Two lessons, no fluff.*

### Lesson 4.1 — Your First $1,000 (8 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a concrete plan to reach her first $1,000 in savings.

Teaching outline:
- Make the case for the first $1,000 specifically: it's the amount that covers most small emergencies (a car repair, a vet bill) without reaching for a credit card, which is why it comes before debt payoff in priority.
- Address the "but I have debt, shouldn't I pay that first" question directly — a small starter fund prevents new debt while she works the plan from Module 3.
- Give three fast ways to find money for this: a one-time push (selling something, a tax refund, a bonus), a temporary pause on one Life-bucket expense, or a short sprint of extra hours/gig income.
- Show setting up a separate savings account for this money specifically, so it's not sitting in checking where it quietly gets spent.
- Have her set a target date for reaching $1,000 based on a realistic monthly contribution.

**Opening line:** "One thousand dollars, held separately, changes how the whole rest of this plan feels — let's get you there first."

### Lesson 4.2 — Sizing Your Real Emergency Fund (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner knows her full emergency fund target based on her actual monthly needs.

Teaching outline:
- Once the first $1,000 is in place, teach the full emergency fund target: three to six months of her Needs-bucket expenses from Module 2, not her full income.
- Give guidance on where to land in that range: closer to three months for stable dual-income or salaried situations, closer to six for freelance, commission, or single-income households.
- Address the "this feels impossible" reaction directly: this is a multi-month or multi-year goal by design, not something to build overnight, and Module 6 will turn it into a real timeline.
- Cover where to keep this money (a high-yield savings account, separate from spending and separate from the debt payoff plan) so it stays both safe and slightly growing.
- Close by having her calculate her personal three-month and six-month numbers and mark where on that range she's aiming.

**Opening line:** "Your real safety net isn't a number you copy from the internet — it's three to six months of YOUR needs. Let's find yours."

**Workbook — Module 4: The Safety Net**
- First $1,000 plan: current amount saved, monthly contribution, target date, and one funding idea from the lesson she'll actually use.
- Full fund calculator: monthly Needs total (from Module 2) × 3 and × 6, with a line to circle her personal target and where she'll keep it.

## Module 5 — Saving on Purpose

*Framing: saving that depends on willpower fails. This module removes willpower from the equation entirely.*

### Lesson 5.1 — Automating So Willpower Isn't Required (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner has at least one automatic transfer set up so saving happens without a monthly decision.

Teaching outline:
- Open with the core principle: the households that save consistently aren't more disciplined, they've just made saving automatic instead of a decision they have to make and win every single month.
- Walk through setting up an automatic transfer timed to payday, for an amount pulled straight from her Future bucket in Module 2.
- Cover starting small if the ideal amount feels scary — a $25 automatic transfer that actually happens beats a $200 planned transfer that gets skipped.
- Address the psychological shift: once it's automatic, the money is "already saved" before she sees it in checking, removing the temptation to spend it first.
- Have her actually set up the transfer during the lesson, not just plan to later.

**Opening line:** "The goal isn't more willpower, it's needing less of it — let's automate this so it just happens."

### Lesson 5.2 — Sinking Funds for the Life You Actually Want (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner has at least two sinking funds set up for specific, planned future expenses.

Teaching outline:
- Introduce sinking funds as the tool that prevents "surprise" expenses that aren't actually surprises — holiday spending, an annual insurance bill, a trip, a wedding gift season.
- Teach the simple math: take the total cost, divide by the number of months until it's due, and that's the monthly amount to set aside.
- Give common sinking fund categories to spark her own list: holidays/gifts, car maintenance, annual subscriptions or insurance, travel, a personal goal purchase.
- Show keeping sinking funds separate from the emergency fund — the emergency fund is for the unplanned, sinking funds are for the fully planned.
- Have her set up two real sinking funds with target amounts and monthly contributions before moving to Module 6.

**Opening line:** "The 'surprise' expenses every year aren't actually surprises — let's plan for them on purpose."

**Workbook — Module 5: Saving on Purpose**
- Automation setup: amount, source account, destination account, transfer date — confirmed with a checkbox once actually set up.
- Sinking fund planner: a table for at least 3 funds with columns for total cost, months until needed, and monthly contribution.

## Module 6 — Goals With a Deadline

*Framing: closing the course by turning everything built so far into one real map with real dates, so "someday" becomes a plan.*

### Lesson 6.1 — Turning Dreams Into Numbers (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner has translated her top 1–3 money goals into specific dollar amounts and dates.

Teaching outline:
- Open by naming the gap between a wish ("I want to feel financially secure") and a goal (a specific number by a specific date) — this lesson closes that gap.
- Walk through picking her top one to three goals for the coming year, pulling from anything mentioned earlier in the course (finishing the emergency fund, paying off a specific debt, a big purchase, starting an investment habit).
- Teach turning each into the same simple formula: total amount needed, divided by months available, equals the monthly amount required.
- Address realism directly: if the required monthly amount doesn't fit her three-bucket budget from Module 2, the goal needs a longer timeline or the budget needs revisiting — both are fine, guessing isn't.
- Have her write all chosen goals in the exact number-and-date format before the final lesson.

**Opening line:** "A dream with a date and a number on it is just a goal — let's put both on paper."

### Lesson 6.2 — Your One-Year Money Map (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner leaves the course with one visual, month-by-month map connecting everything she's built.

Teaching outline:
- Bring together every piece from the course into one page: current snapshot, three-bucket budget, debt payoff order, emergency fund target, sinking funds, and the goals from 6.1.
- Walk through filling in a twelve-month grid with key milestones per month (e.g., "month 3: emergency fund hits $1,000," "month 7: card X paid off").
- Address the emotional close of the course: this map will need adjusting as real life happens, and that's not failure — it's what a living plan does.
- Give a simple monthly check-in habit to keep the map current (same five-minute spirit as the numbers review in the flagship course), so this doesn't become another document that gets made once and forgotten.
- Close by connecting back to Module 1: she started by finally looking at the number without flinching, and she's ending with a full map she built herself.

**Opening line:** "Let's put the whole year on one page — everything you've built in this course, connected into a single map."

**Workbook — Module 6: Goals With a Deadline**
- Goals worksheet: up to 3 goals, each with total amount, target date, and required monthly amount.
- One-year money map: a 12-month grid with a line per month for the one milestone she's aiming for.
- Monthly check-in template: a simple repeatable box (date, what's on track, what needs adjusting) to photocopy or reuse each month after the course ends.
