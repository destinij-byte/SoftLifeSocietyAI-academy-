# Build Your Digital Empire — Full Curriculum

**Price:** $149 · **Tier:** Single course · **Modules:** 7 · **Lessons:** 16

**The promise:** by the end of this course, a learner has taken one skill she already has and turned it into a digital product she can sell — with a name, a home online, a price, and a way to launch it — without needing a team, a following, or a business degree.

**Who it's for:** a woman with a skill, a following, or a point of view, who has been circling "I should sell something" for months without a clear next step.

This document is the recording and workbook script for the course. Each lesson lists what to actually teach on camera (not word-for-word, but everything a script would need), a suggested opening line to say straight to camera, and the matching workbook page a learner fills in after watching. Workbook pages are grouped by module, the way they'll sit in the downloadable PDF.

## Module 1 — The Idea Layer

*Framing: most women don't have an idea problem, they have a decision problem — too many half-ideas and no way to pick one. This module is entirely about narrowing.*

### Lesson 1.1 — Turning a Skill Into a Sellable Product (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner can name three of her own skills and recognize which ones are already "sellable" versus which need translating into a result.

Teaching outline:
- Open with the core reframe: a sellable product isn't a new skill, it's an existing skill made transferable — something she already does that someone else would pay to shortcut.
- Walk through the three sources most first products come from: something people already ask her for, something she had to teach herself the hard way, and something she does faster or better than most people around her.
- Give the test for whether a skill is "sellable" yet: can she describe the RESULT it gets someone, not just the activity itself (not "I'm good at budgeting," but "I get people to their first $1,000 saved in 60 days").
- Run through 3–4 real-feeling examples across different skill types (organizing, cooking, systems, styling, parenting logistics) so no viewer feels like this only works for "online business" skills.
- Close by having her write down every skill she can think of in two minutes, no filtering yet — filtering is lesson 1.3's job.

**Opening line:** "You don't need a new skill to build this — you need to stop underpricing the one you already have."

### Lesson 1.2 — Finding the Woman You're Building For (8 min)

**Objective:** the learner can describe her ideal buyer as a specific person with a specific problem, not a demographic.

Teaching outline:
- Name the mistake: building the product before deciding who it's for, which is why so many first products feel like they're for "everyone" and sell to no one.
- Teach the difference between a demographic ("women 25–40") and a buyer ("a woman who just got promoted and has no idea how to budget on a bigger paycheck") — the second one you can actually write a sales page to.
- Give three questions to find her: who was I before I learned this? Who do I already talk to about this for free? Whose version of this problem do I understand best?
- Address the fear directly: narrowing the buyer doesn't shrink the market, it sharpens the message — a product "for everyone" reads as a product for no one.
- Have her write one sentence: "I help ______ do ______ so they can ______."

**Opening line:** "The riches are in the specifics — let's find the one woman you're actually talking to."

### Lesson 1.3 — Naming and Positioning Your Offer (12 min)

**Objective:** the learner leaves with a working name and a one-line positioning statement for her offer.

Teaching outline:
- Bring back her list from 1.1 and her buyer from 1.2, and show how to cross them to land on the one idea worth building first.
- Give the naming framework: name it for the transformation, not the format — "The 60-Day Save" beats "My Budgeting Guide."
- Cover the three naming traps to avoid: too clever (no one remembers it), too generic (sounds like everyone else's), too jargon-y (buyer has to ask what it means).
- Teach the one-line positioning formula: "[Product name] helps [buyer] go from [before state] to [after state] in [timeframe/format]."
- Have her write three name options and pressure-test each against the positioning line before picking one to move forward with in Module 2.

**Opening line:** "A great name does half your selling for you — here's how to find yours."

**Workbook — Module 1: The Idea Layer**
- Skill inventory: three columns to fill in — "Things people already ask me for," "Things I taught myself the hard way," "Things I do faster than most people I know."
- Sellable-skill test: for each skill listed, a line to write the RESULT it gets someone (not the activity).
- Buyer sketch: name, age range, one sentence on where she is in life, one sentence on the specific problem she has right now.
- The one-line formula, fill-in-the-blank: "I help ______ do ______ so they can ______."
- Naming shortlist: three name options, plus a checkbox next to each for "passes the positioning test."

## Module 2 — Your Digital Product

*Framing: this is where most people either freeze (too many format options) or over-build (a 40-page guide when a 6-page one would sell). The module's job is to make the format decision fast and the build process faster.*

### Lesson 2.1 — Choosing the Right Format (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner picks one product format based on her buyer's habits, not her own preference.

Teaching outline:
- List the five most common first-product formats: PDF guide/workbook, templates, a short video course, a checklist/toolkit, a done-with-you notion or spreadsheet system.
- Teach the real deciding factor: not "what am I most comfortable making" but "how does my buyer already like to consume things" — a busy mom wants a checklist, not a 90-minute video.
- Cover the "smallest viable version" idea: the first product should be the smallest thing that still fully delivers the promise from Module 1 — bigger can come later as an upsell.
- Warn against the two most common format mistakes: building a course when a template would do, and building a template when the buyer actually needs to be walked through something.
- Have her commit to one format in writing before the next lesson.

**Opening line:** "The best first product is the smallest one that still keeps your promise."

### Lesson 2.2 — Outlining It in One Sitting (14 min)

**Objective:** the learner leaves with a complete outline of her product's content, start to finish.

Teaching outline:
- Introduce the "outline before you open a design tool" rule — most stalled products stalled because someone opened Canva before they knew what the pages said.
- Teach the simple three-part shape almost every first product follows: where the buyer starts (the problem, named back to her), the method (the steps, in order), and where she ends up (the result, plus what's next).
- Model turning the positioning line from Lesson 1.3 into a table of contents — each "step" in the method becomes a section or module.
- Give the 20-minute timer trick: set a timer, write section headers and one sentence per section with no editing, to get the whole shape down before perfectionism sets in.
- Address scope creep directly: if a section doesn't serve the ONE promise, cut it — it can become a future product.

**Opening line:** "You can build this in one sitting if you outline it before you decorate it."

### Lesson 2.3 — Building It in Canva and Notion (18 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a first draft of her actual product file, built from the outline.

Teaching outline:
- Screen-share style walkthrough: open Canva and Notion side by side, and show turning yesterday's outline into real pages — this is the one lesson that should feel like sitting next to her while she builds.
- Cover picking a Canva template that matches her brand quickly rather than starting from a blank page (link to a few pre-vetted templates in the resources).
- Show the actual build order: cover page, table of contents, then working straight down her outline section by section, dropping in placeholder text first and refining second.
- Demonstrate exporting a clean, on-brand PDF from Canva, and — for tool-based products — setting up the matching Notion template with the same structure.
- Close with a realistic timeline: this lesson is the longest because the build itself takes real focused time; encourage her to block 2–3 hours rather than expecting to finish while watching.

**Opening line:** "Open Canva and Notion beside me. We build the actual deliverable — cover, contents, worksheets — in one sitting, then export it the way buyers expect to receive it."

**Workbook — Module 2: Your Digital Product**
- Format decision: five formats listed with a line under each for "why this would/wouldn't fit my buyer," ending in one circled choice.
- One-sitting outline template: numbered section slots (start with 5) with a line each for section title and one-sentence summary.
- Build checklist: cover page, table of contents, each outlined section, worksheets/templates included, export format — a checkbox next to each.
- "Cut list": a space to note anything tempting to add that doesn't serve the one core promise, to save for a future product instead.

## Module 3 — The Home You Own

*Framing: a product needs a home that isn't a social platform's algorithm. This module gets her a site and a sales page she owns.*

### Lesson 3.1 — Setting Up a Site That Sells (16 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a live, simple website or storefront page with her product ready to list.

Teaching outline:
- Reframe the goal: not a full website, just one page that can take a payment — perfection here is the enemy of launching.
- Compare the two realistic paths for this course's audience: a no-code storefront/link-in-bio-plus-checkout tool for the fastest launch, versus a simple site builder for someone who wants more brand control — give a clear recommendation for "if you've never done this before."
- Walk through the essential page elements in order: headline, one clear image or mockup of the product, the price and buy button, and a short trust element (who she is, why she's credible on this).
- Cover the technical minimum: connecting a payment processor, testing a $1 purchase to confirm the flow works, and where the digital file actually gets delivered after purchase.
- Set the standard: this page does not need to be beautiful today, it needs to be able to take money today.

**Opening line:** "You don't need a website, you need one page that can take a payment — let's build that today."

### Lesson 3.2 — Writing a Sales Page in Your Voice (13 min)

**Objective:** the learner has full sales page copy written, in her own voice, following a proven structure.

Teaching outline:
- Give the sales page skeleton: a headline naming the transformation, three to five bullets on what's inside, a short "this is for you if" section, the price with what's included, and an FAQ addressing the top two hesitations.
- Teach writing "in her voice" as translating the positioning line from Module 1 into how she actually talks — read two contrasting examples, one generic and one voice-driven, so the difference is audible.
- Cover objection-handling directly: name the real hesitation a buyer has ("will this actually work for someone like me") and answer it in the FAQ instead of hoping it doesn't come up.
- Give a fill-in-the-blank first draft process: write the headline last, not first — pull it from whatever sentence in her rough draft feels most true.
- Close with a rule: the page is done when it sounds like something she'd actually say out loud to a friend, not like marketing copy.

**Opening line:** "A sales page isn't a pitch, it's you explaining this to a friend who's exactly the person you built it for."

**Workbook — Module 3: The Home You Own**
- Platform decision worksheet: two columns (storefront tool vs. site builder) with space to note which fits her comfort level and timeline, ending in one choice.
- Page-element checklist: headline, product image, price and buy button, credibility line, tested purchase — checkboxes for each as she builds.
- Sales page skeleton: headline, 3–5 bullets, "this is for you if," price/what's included, FAQ (2 questions minimum) — fill-in sections in order.
- Voice check: a space to paste her draft headline and rewrite it as if she were texting a friend, side by side.

## Module 4 — Getting Paid

*Framing: pricing is where confidence and logistics collide. This module handles both — the number, and the actual mechanics of collecting it.*

### Lesson 4.1 — Pricing Without Apologising (11 min)

**Objective:** the learner sets a specific launch price and can explain why, without hedging.

Teaching outline:
- Open with the pattern to break: pricing based on what feels comfortable to charge rather than what the transformation is actually worth.
- Give three pricing anchors to consider: what similar products in her space charge, what a client would pay her for this 1:1, and the $120–$160 range that tends to work for a focused single product like this one.
- Address the two fears directly — "no one will pay that" and "what if I priced it wrong" — and normalize adjusting price after launch based on real data, not guessing forever before starting.
- Teach the psychology of round vs. non-round pricing briefly (e.g., $149 vs. $150) and let her choose what fits her brand.
- Have her commit to a launch price on the page, out loud, before moving on.

**Opening line:** "Say the price out loud before you type it — if you flinch, we're fixing that flinch, not the price."

### Lesson 4.2 — Checkout, Delivery and Receipts (15 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a fully tested purchase-to-delivery flow, with no manual steps required on her end.

Teaching outline:
- Map the full buyer journey after "buy now": payment, confirmation email, product delivery, receipt — and show where each of those actually happens in her chosen tool from Module 3.
- Walk through setting up automatic product delivery (download link or access grant) so she is never manually emailing files to buyers.
- Cover receipts and basic bookkeeping hygiene: keeping a simple record of every sale for tax purposes, even at small volume.
- Show how to test the entire flow herself with a real transaction before telling anyone the product exists.
- Set the expectation: if any step of this requires her to do something by hand every time someone buys, it isn't finished yet.

**Opening line:** "If you have to manually do anything every time someone buys, this lesson isn't done yet — let's automate the whole handoff."

**Workbook — Module 4: Getting Paid**
- Pricing worksheet: three anchor prices (competitor, 1:1 rate, category range) with her final launch price circled and one sentence on why.
- Delivery flow map: five steps (payment → confirmation → delivery → receipt → records) with a checkbox confirming each is automated.
- Test-purchase log: date, amount, what worked, what needs fixing before real launch.

## Module 5 — Content That Sells Softly

*Framing: this is not a marketing bootcamp — it's a sustainable rhythm that doesn't require being "on" all day.*

### Lesson 5.1 — The Three-Post Rhythm (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner can plan a week of content using three repeatable post types.

Teaching outline:
- Introduce the three post types that do almost all the work: the problem post (naming the pain point her buyer has), the proof post (a result, testimonial, or behind-the-scenes of the product working), and the invitation post (a direct, low-pressure call to check out the offer).
- Show real example captions for each type, written in the "soft" tone — confident, warm, no hustle-culture pressure language.
- Teach the ratio that keeps it sustainable: roughly two problem/proof posts for every one direct invitation, so the audience doesn't feel sold to constantly.
- Cover format flexibility: the same three post types work as a caption, a short video script, or a carousel — the type matters more than the format.
- Have her draft one of each type for her actual offer before the next lesson.

**Opening line:** "You only need three kinds of posts — let's make sure you never run out of what to say."

### Lesson 5.2 — Batching a Month in a Day (12 min)

**Objective:** the learner leaves with a month of content mapped and a batching system she can repeat.

Teaching outline:
- Make the case for batching: content posted "in the moment" is where burnout comes from — batching separates creating from showing up.
- Walk through a simple monthly content calendar using the three-post rhythm from 5.1, filling four weeks in about twenty minutes.
- Give a lightweight batching process: write all captions first in one sitting, then record/design visuals in a second sitting, then schedule everything in a third.
- Recommend a basic scheduling tool so posting doesn't depend on remembering to do it live every day.
- Close with the permission slip: a month planned in a day means the rest of the month is free to actually run the business.

**Opening line:** "One focused day can cover your whole month — here's exactly how to structure it."

**Workbook — Module 5: Content That Sells Softly**
- Three-post-type worksheet: a blank draft slot for one problem post, one proof post, and one invitation post.
- Monthly calendar grid: four weeks, three post slots per week, pre-labeled with post type so the rhythm is built in.
- Batching schedule: three time blocks to fill in (write captions, create visuals, schedule) with target dates.

## Module 6 — The Quiet Launch

*Framing: a launch that doesn't require becoming a different, louder person — just a plan and some warm-up.*

### Lesson 6.1 — Your Seven-Day Launch Plan (17 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a day-by-day plan for launch week.

Teaching outline:
- Reframe "launch" as a week-long conversation, not a single announcement post — most sales come from the follow-up, not the first mention.
- Walk through a full seven-day skeleton: days 1–2 warm-up (teasing the problem, no offer yet), day 3 the announcement, days 4–5 proof and FAQs, day 6 a direct case for buying now, day 7 last-call and close.
- Cover what to do on each day in practical terms: which post type from Module 5 fits, whether it's a story, a post, or an email.
- Address launch-week nerves directly: quiet doesn't mean invisible — it means steady and repeated, not loud and one-time.
- Have her fill in her own seven-day calendar with real dates before the module ends.

**Opening line:** "A launch isn't one big moment, it's seven small ones — let's map yours."

### Lesson 6.2 — Warming Your List Before You Ask (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a plan to engage her existing audience or email list before asking them to buy.

Teaching outline:
- Teach the core idea: people buy from someone they feel is already talking to them, not someone who shows up once to sell.
- Give three low-effort warm-up moves: a poll or question sticker about the problem her product solves, sharing a personal story connected to why she built it, and a "coming soon" mention with no link, just curiosity.
- Cover list-building basics for anyone starting from zero: a simple lead magnet or waitlist so there's at least a small warm audience by launch day.
- Address the size myth directly: a warm list of 50 people who trust her outsells a cold list of 5,000 who don't.
- Close by having her schedule at least one warm-up touchpoint before day 3 of her launch plan.

**Opening line:** "Before you ask anyone to buy, make sure they already feel like you've been talking to them."

**Workbook — Module 6: The Quiet Launch**
- Seven-day launch calendar: days 1–7, each with a line for the day's theme and the specific post/email planned.
- Warm-up tracker: three planned touchpoints (poll/question, personal story, coming-soon mention) with target dates.
- List-building note: current list/audience size, and one action to grow it before launch if starting small.

## Module 7 — Soft Systems

*Framing: the module that makes this a business instead of a one-time project — the parts that run without her.*

### Lesson 7.1 — Automating Delivery and Emails (14 min)

**Objective:** the learner has automated the emails a buyer receives after purchase, without writing them one by one.

Teaching outline:
- Revisit the delivery flow from Module 4 and layer in email automation: a welcome/how-to-use email, an inactivity nudge for buyers who haven't started, and a completion email with a next-offer recommendation.
- Show setting up these three emails once inside her existing email tool, connected to the purchase or course-completion trigger, so they send themselves.
- Cover writing each email briefly and warmly — this should sound like her checking in, not an automated corporate sequence.
- Address the "I don't have an email tool yet" case with a simple, low-cost starting recommendation.
- Set the finish line: once these three emails are live, every future buyer gets the same thoughtful experience without her doing anything.

**Opening line:** "Let's make sure every buyer gets a warm welcome and a nudge to finish — without you writing a single email by hand."

### Lesson 7.2 — Reading Your Numbers Once a Month (8 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a simple monthly check-in routine for her product's performance.

Teaching outline:
- Introduce the short list of numbers that actually matter at this stage: sales this month, completion rate (are buyers finishing it), and where sales are coming from (which platform, which post).
- Teach a five-minute monthly review process rather than obsessive daily checking — sustainability over hustle.
- Show how to use what she learns: doubling down on whichever content type is actually driving sales, and revisiting pricing or the sales page if completion or conversion is low.
- Address the emotional side: a slow month is data, not a verdict — the review is there to inform the next month, not to spiral about the last one.
- Close the course by connecting back to Module 1: she now owns a real, sellable, systemized product — and the numbers review is what keeps it improving on its own schedule, not hers every day.

**Opening line:** "Once a month, five minutes — that's all it takes to keep this business honest and improving."

**Workbook — Module 7: Soft Systems**
- Email automation checklist: welcome email, inactivity nudge, completion email — each with a checkbox and a one-line note on its trigger.
- Monthly numbers log: a repeatable table with columns for month, sales, completion rate, and top traffic source, with 3 blank rows to start filling in after launch.
- Course close-out: a short reflection prompt — "the one thing I'm proudest I built in this course is ___" and "the next product I might build is ___."
