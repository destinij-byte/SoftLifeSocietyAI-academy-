**The promise:** by the end of this course, a learner has a working AI toolkit she actually uses — for planning her week, writing content in her own voice, handling the admin she dreads, and running her household — plus a clear sense of where AI stops and her own judgment has to take over.

## Module 1 — Getting Comfortable With AI

*Framing: most of the hesitation here isn't about capability, it's about not knowing where to start. This module removes the intimidation before asking her to do anything.*

### Lesson 1.1 — What These Tools Actually Do (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner can explain, in plain language, what an AI chat tool is actually doing and where its limits are.

Teaching outline:
- Open by naming the two extremes people land on — "it's magic, it knows everything" or "it's useless, it gave me a weird answer once" — and reset expectations to the middle, honest truth.
- Explain simply what these tools are good at (organizing, drafting, brainstorming, summarizing, explaining) versus what they get wrong (specific facts, anything time-sensitive, anything requiring real judgment about her actual life).
- Teach the single most useful mental model: it's a very well-read assistant who's never met her and needs to be told everything relevant, every time — it doesn't remember unless she tells it to.
- Address the guilt/fear angle directly and briefly: using AI isn't cheating or lazy, it's using a tool, the same way a calculator didn't make people bad at math judgment.
- Close with a live, no-pressure first prompt she can try during the lesson just to get a feel for the interface.

**Opening line:** "Let's demystify this in nine minutes — no jargon, just what it actually does and doesn't do."

### Lesson 1.2 — Setting Up Your AI Toolkit (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner has one AI tool set up and ready to use, with an account and the basics configured.

Teaching outline:
- Recommend one clear starting tool rather than overwhelming with five options — pick based on what's genuinely easiest for a first-timer, and mention that the skills transfer to any tool later.
- Walk through account setup step by step: creating an account, understanding the free vs. paid tiers, and what's actually worth paying for at this stage (usually: nothing yet, start free).
- Cover the interface basics: where to type, how to start a "new chat" versus continuing one, and why starting fresh chats for different topics keeps things cleaner.
- Introduce the idea of saving good prompts she'll reuse (a simple running note is enough — no special tool required).
- Close with her toolkit fully set up and one practice conversation started, ready for Module 2's real use cases.

**Opening line:** "Fifteen minutes from now, you'll have this set up and ready — let's do it together, live."

**Workbook — Module 1: Getting Comfortable With AI**
- Mental model recap: a one-line fill-in — "The one thing I need to remember is: AI is ___, not ___."
- Setup checklist: account created, free tier understood, first practice chat started — checkboxes for each.
- Prompt journal starter page: a simple two-column table (prompt / how it went) to start collecting what works for her.

## Module 2 — Reclaiming Your Calendar

*Framing: the fastest, most satisfying win in the whole course — getting a week's worth of planning done in minutes instead of an hour of staring at a blank page.*

### Lesson 2.1 — Prompting a Weekly Plan in Minutes (11 min)

**Objective:** the learner can generate a usable weekly plan by giving AI the right context.

Teaching outline:
- Open with the common failure mode: typing "help me plan my week" and getting a generic, useless answer — then show why (not enough context given).
- Teach the context formula: what's already fixed on the calendar, top 3 priorities for the week, and any constraints (energy levels, specific days that are tighter than others).
- Model a full prompt live, then show the output and how to react to it — treating the AI's plan as a rough draft to edit, not a final answer to accept blindly.
- Cover asking follow-up questions to refine it ("move my deep work to mornings," "I forgot I have an appointment Tuesday") rather than starting over from scratch each time.
- Have her run this for her actual upcoming week during the lesson.

**Opening line:** "Give it the right details and this turns into a real planning assistant — let's build your week right now."

### Lesson 2.2 — Building Your Personal Assistant Prompt (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a saved, reusable "personal assistant" prompt template she can use every week going forward.

Teaching outline:
- Introduce the idea of a template prompt: instead of rebuilding context every time, she writes it once and reuses it with small updates.
- Walk through building the template together: a fixed opening describing her role/life context, a section for that week's specific priorities, and a closing instruction on the format she wants back (a simple list, a table, time-blocked).
- Show saving this template somewhere easy to grab (notes app, a doc) so using it becomes a five-second copy-paste, not a rewrite.
- Address personalization: everyone's template should look a little different based on what actually matters to her (a business owner's looks different from a stay-at-home parent's).
- Close with her final template saved and ready to reuse starting next week.

**Opening line:** "Let's write this once, so every future week only takes you thirty seconds to plan."

**Workbook — Module 2: Reclaiming Your Calendar**
- Weekly plan prompt worksheet: fixed calendar items, top 3 priorities, constraints — the exact fields to fill in before prompting.
- Personal assistant template: a fill-in-the-blank template structure (role/context, this week's priorities, desired output format) to copy into her AI tool going forward.

## Module 3 — Content Without the Burnout

*Framing: not "let AI write your content" but "let AI clear the blank page so your real voice can show up faster."*

### Lesson 3.1 — Turning One Idea Into a Week of Posts (12 min)

**Objective:** the learner can take a single idea and generate a week's worth of content angles from it.

Teaching outline:
- Open with the real bottleneck in content creation: it's rarely a lack of ideas, it's turning one idea into enough different angles to post about all week without repeating herself.
- Teach the "one idea, five angles" prompt approach: feeding AI one core idea or story and asking for it back as a tip post, a personal story post, a myth-busting post, a question/engagement post, and a behind-the-scenes post.
- Model this live with a realistic example idea, showing the range of outputs from one input.
- Cover treating every output as a rough draft — the next lesson (3.2) is specifically about making sure it doesn't sound robotic once she edits it.
- Have her run this process on one of her own real content ideas before the lesson ends.

**Opening line:** "One idea, five posts — let's stop staring at a blank content calendar for good."

### Lesson 3.2 — Finding Your Voice So It Doesn't Sound Like a Robot (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner can reliably edit AI-generated drafts so they sound like her, not like generic AI copy.

Teaching outline:
- Name the tell-tale signs of "AI voice" directly so she can spot it: overly formal transitions, excessive emoji or hashtags, phrases that sound like nobody actually talks, a lack of specific personal detail.
- Teach the fix: feeding AI a sample of her own past writing or captions and asking it to match that tone specifically, rather than accepting generic default tone.
- Give a simple personal edit pass to always do: read it out loud, and change any sentence she wouldn't actually say to a friend.
- Cover adding one true personal detail to every AI-drafted post — a specific example, a real number, an actual moment — since specificity is what generic AI text is missing.
- Close by having her take one draft from Lesson 3.1 through this full "make it sound like me" process.

**Opening line:** "Here's exactly how to spot the robot voice — and three small edits that make any draft sound like you."

**Workbook — Module 3: Content Without the Burnout**
- One idea, five angles worksheet: her core idea at the top, five labeled slots for each angle's draft.
- Voice-check pass: a short checklist — read aloud, replace robotic phrases, add one true detail — applied to one real post before posting it.

## Module 4 — The Admin You Hate, Automated

*Framing: the unglamorous, high-relief part of the course — the tasks she's been dreading get a system.*

### Lesson 4.1 — Email and Inbox Triage (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a repeatable AI-assisted process for clearing a backlogged inbox.

Teaching outline:
- Open by naming the real cost of an overflowing inbox: not just the mess, but the mental weight of every unanswered message sitting there.
- Teach a triage prompt approach: pasting in a batch of email content (with any sensitive info removed) and asking AI to summarize what each needs and draft short reply options.
- Cover using AI to draft replies she then reviews and personalizes rather than sending untouched — speed without losing her own voice or accuracy.
- Address the privacy note directly and clearly: never paste in truly sensitive personal, financial, or client information — this tool is for drafting language, not for handling private data.
- Have her run this process on her actual current inbox backlog during the lesson.

**Opening line:** "Let's finally clear that inbox — with AI doing the first draft and you doing the thirty-second edit."

### Lesson 4.2 — Templates for the Messages You Send Weekly (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a small library of message templates for the emails/texts she sends most often.

Teaching outline:
- Introduce the idea: most people retype some version of the same 5–8 messages constantly (a follow-up, a scheduling message, a polite decline, a check-in) — these deserve permanent templates.
- Walk through identifying her own most repeated message types by scanning her sent folder or just thinking through the week.
- Model generating a clean, warm, reusable template for each one with AI, then personalizing the tone to sound like her.
- Cover storing these somewhere she'll actually find them again (notes app, saved email drafts) so the system gets used, not built once and forgotten.
- Close with at least three real templates finished and saved.

**Opening line:** "You're probably retyping the same five messages every week — let's turn them into templates, once, for good."

**Workbook — Module 4: The Admin You Hate, Automated**
- Inbox triage log: a simple table (sender/topic, what's needed, drafted reply status) to run through a real backlog.
- Message template library: a table with columns for message type, when it's used, and the finished template text — with room for at least 5 entries.

## Module 5 — AI for the Home and Family

*Framing: taking the same skills out of "work mode" and into the parts of life that eat the most invisible mental energy.*

### Lesson 5.1 — Meal Planning and Grocery Lists in Minutes (10 min)

**Objective:** the learner can generate a full week of meals and a matching grocery list using AI.

Teaching outline:
- Open by naming meal planning as one of the most draining recurring decisions in a household — not because cooking is hard, but because deciding what to cook, every day, is exhausting.
- Teach the context-rich prompt: household size, dietary needs or preferences, roughly how much time is available to cook on weeknights versus weekends, and what's already in the pantry if relevant.
- Model generating a full week of dinners plus a consolidated grocery list organized by store section.
- Cover quick follow-up adjustments (swap a meal, account for a busy Tuesday, use up something about to expire) rather than regenerating the whole week.
- Have her generate her actual meal plan for the coming week during the lesson.

**Opening line:** "What's for dinner this week? Let's get that answered for all seven nights in the next ten minutes."

### Lesson 5.2 — Organizing Family Schedules and To-Dos (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a system for using AI to untangle a busy household's schedule and task list.

Teaching outline:
- Open with the mental load reality: she's often the one holding every family member's schedule in her head, and that invisible tracking is exhausting even when nothing's technically going wrong.
- Teach feeding AI everyone's fixed commitments and open tasks to get back an organized view: what's happening when, what needs to be done by when, and any scheduling conflicts flagged.
- Cover using it for specific recurring headaches: packing lists for trips, holiday planning, coordinating multiple kids' activities, or household project checklists.
- Address the boundary clearly: AI can organize the information she gives it, but she's still the one deciding priorities — it's a mental-load reducer, not a replacement for her judgment about her own family.
- Close with her building one real organizing system (a schedule view or a checklist) for something actually on her plate this month.

**Opening line:** "You shouldn't have to hold the whole family's schedule in your head alone — let's get some of that weight off you."

**Workbook — Module 5: AI for the Home and Family**
- Meal plan worksheet: household size, dietary notes, time available, pantry staples — the fields to fill in before prompting, plus space to paste her finished week's plan.
- Family organizer: a space to list fixed commitments and open to-dos for the household, plus one specific upcoming event (trip, holiday, project) to build a checklist for.

## Module 6 — Guardrails and Good Judgment

*Framing: closing the course with the most important lesson — knowing where the tool ends and she begins.*

### Lesson 6.1 — What Not to Hand Over to AI (9 min)

**Objective:** the learner can name specific categories of decisions and information she will keep fully in her own hands.

Teaching outline:
- Open by reinforcing that everything in this course has been about removing drudgery, never about removing her judgment — this lesson draws that line explicitly.
- Cover categories to never fully hand over: final decisions involving other people's wellbeing (medical, legal, financial decisions for herself or family), anything requiring truly private or sensitive information, and any message or decision where getting it wrong has real consequences.
- Teach the "draft, never decide" rule as a simple heuristic she can apply to any new use case that comes up after the course ends.
- Give a few real examples of AI getting confidently wrong answers, so she leaves appropriately skeptical rather than over-trusting.
- Close by having her write her own personal list of "always mine to decide" categories.

**Opening line:** "This tool is here to save you time, never to make your calls for you — let's draw that line clearly."

### Lesson 6.2 — Fact-Checking and Staying You (8 min)

**Objective:** the learner has a habit of verifying anything factual before using it, and feels confident her AI-assisted output still sounds like her.

Teaching outline:
- Teach a simple rule: anything presented as a specific fact, statistic, date, or claim gets independently verified before she repeats it anywhere public.
- Cover quick verification habits: a fast search to confirm anything factual, and being especially cautious with anything time-sensitive since these tools can be out of date.
- Revisit the "sounding like me" work from Module 3 as an ongoing habit, not a one-time lesson — a quick gut-check before posting or sending anything AI-assisted.
- Close the course by reflecting on the shift from Lesson 1.1's hesitation to a working toolkit she now owns, and encourage her to keep a running list of new use cases as she discovers them in daily life.
- End with an invitation to revisit this course's workbook anytime as a reference, not just a one-time watch-through.

**Opening line:** "Last thing, and it matters most: always double-check the facts, and always make sure the final version still sounds like you."

**Workbook — Module 6: Guardrails and Good Judgment**
- "Always mine to decide" list: a space to write out her own personal boundaries for what she'll never fully hand over.
- Fact-check habit card: a short repeatable checklist — verify facts independently, double-check anything time-sensitive, read the final version aloud to confirm it sounds like her.
- Course close-out: "the use case I'm most excited to try this week is ___."
